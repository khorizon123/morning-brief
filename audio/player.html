Trump Rebuilds Tariff Wall With New Duties on 60+ Countries

The US replaced its expiring 10% global tariff with new levies of 10–12.5% on roughly 60–80 trading partners including Mexico, the EU, Japan, and Australia, justified as a crackdown on forced-labor supply chains under Section 301 authority. Exemptions cover oil, gas, fertilizer, USMCA-covered goods, and items already hit by national-security tariffs. Australia, Singapore, and China publicly objected, while the EU said the move matched last year's trade agreement.

Trump's original "Liberation Day" tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court in February, forcing the administration to cycle through different legal justifications — first a temporary global levy, now this forced-labor rationale — to keep tariff pressure in place.

Oil Tops $100 as Trump Weighs 'Massive Attack' on Iran, Houthis Widen Red Sea Front

Brent crude spiked above $100 a barrel for the first time since May after Houthi militants struck two Saudi oil tankers (the Encelia and Layla) in the Red Sea. Trump told Axios he was considering a "massive attack" on Iran and would hold Tehran responsible for further Houthi strikes. A global bond sell-off intensified as investors priced in a fresh inflation shock, pushing borrowing costs to their highest levels in over a year.

This is now the third front squeezing world oil supply — alongside the blockaded Strait of Hormuz and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries — reviving fears of a 1970s-style stagflation shock just as the Iran war enters its 12th consecutive night of US strikes.

Trump Puts New Saudi Nuclear Deal in Doubt With Israel-Recognition Demand

A day after signing a US-Saudi civilian nuclear cooperation agreement, Trump added a new condition: the deal is "totally subject to" Saudi Arabia establishing diplomatic relations with Israel. Riyadh has long said it won't normalize ties without a credible pathway to Palestinian statehood, potentially unraveling the whole arrangement. Iran, separately, rejected a US cease-fire proposal delivered via the Iraqi prime minister.

The original deal had already drawn criticism for lacking the strict "gold standard" non-proliferation safeguards the US required of the UAE in 2009, raising fears it could let Riyadh eventually enrich its own uranium.
