Venezuela Nears Deal to Move $4bn Gold Reserve From London to New York

Venezuela's interim government and opposition are close to an agreement letting Delcy Rodríguez's administration gain legal control over 31 tonnes of central bank gold held at the Bank of England, without immediate rights to sell it. The gold could instead serve as collateral for borrowing to fund reconstruction after June's deadly earthquakes. It's one of the first concrete results of a US-backed negotiation process between the government and opposition.

The gold has been frozen since 2019, when the UK and US recognized opposition figure Juan Guaidó rather than Nicolás Maduro as Venezuela's legitimate president, letting the opposition claim control of the bullion.

Trump Administration Approves $24.3bn F-35 Sale to Saudi Arabia Amid Espionage Fears

The Trump administration approved a potential sale of dozens of F-35 fighter jets to Saudi Arabia, worth roughly $24.3bn, fulfilling a request Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman made last year. Congress must still approve the sale, and US intelligence has warned that China—which maintains security ties with Riyadh—could acquire or steal the jet's technology. The deal comes as Saudi Arabia faces a deepening crisis from the Houthis' Red Sea offensive.

The F-35 is America's premier stealth fighter, and Washington has historically restricted its sale to close allies over fears sensitive technology could leak to rivals like China or Russia.

Sweden's Center-Left Wins Election as Kristersson Resigns

Sweden's center-right prime minister Ulf Kristersson resigned after his coalition—which included the hard-right, Sweden Democrats—was narrowly defeated in Sunday's election. Preliminary counts give Magdalena Andersson's Social Democrats and allies 176 of parliament's 349 seats, positioning her to become the next prime minister and form what will likely be a delicate coalition.

The result makes Sweden one of the first European countries to reverse the continent's recent drift toward hard-right governments.

UN Investigators: US Airstrikes on Iran Likely War Crimes

A UN fact-finding mission concluded there are "reasonable grounds" to believe the US committed war crimes in Iran, citing February strikes on a primary school in Minab and a sports complex in Lamerd that killed more than 170 civilians. The State Department rejected the findings; Washington has denied hitting Lamerd and says it's investigating the Minab incident.

The strikes occurred during the US-Iran conflict that began earlier this year and has since driven a surge in oil prices and a Houthi offensive in the Red Sea.

The Bank of Japan lifted its policy rate a quarter point to 1.25%, its first hike since June, as core consumer prices rose 1.7% year-on-year in August. The move is meant to relieve pressure on the yen, which Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been propping up alongside Tokyo since a joint intervention in late July, but the currency weakened again after the Fed's own hike this week. Governor Ueda Kazuo warned inflation could still overshoot the bank's 2% target.

FAA to Launch AI Air-Traffic Control Tool Starting in D.C.

The FAA is preparing to debut Smart (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories), an AI system meant to reduce flight delays by analyzing airline schedules, weather and runway capacity to predict traffic flows and flag conflicts before they happen. It launches in the Washington, D.C. corridor as soon as Monday ahead of a nationwide rollout. Air Space Intelligence won an $875 million, 12-year contract to build the system, which is intended to function as something like the brains of US air-traffic control.

Vy Capital

Vy Capital is a famously low-profile venture capital firm, run by Alexander Tamas and John Hering, that has quietly become one of Elon Musk's largest financial backers. Through years of gradual, opportunistic buying, the firm has built a stake in SpaceX now worth roughly $40 billion, alongside earlier bets on companies like Revolut and Nubank. Despite that scale, Vy keeps almost no public profile — no flashy press, no thought-leadership circuit — a deliberate contrast to Silicon Valley's usual attention-seeking venture culture.
